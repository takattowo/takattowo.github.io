Impostor syndrome in the AI age

2026-03-24 12 revisions

The other day I shipped something I was actually proud of. It solved a real problem, the code was clean, the writeup was tight. About an hour later I caught myself thinking, "yeah, but how much of that was me?" Not in a humble-brag way. More like a quiet, slightly cold question.

I'd used AI at three or four points along the way. Nothing crazy. A draft of a function, a reworded paragraph, a sanity check on an edge case. The work was still mine. I made the calls. But the question stuck around anyway. If I needed the assistant to get there, do I get to call it mine?

It's a new kind of impostor syndrome, and I don't think the usual advice really covers it. So I want to write about what it is, what the research says, and what I've started doing to keep my head on straight.

Quick disclosure: yes, I used AI on this post, mostly for rewording and grammar. The ideas, the research, and the opinions are mine.

The classic version, and what AI changed

Impostor syndrome in tech was bad way before AI showed up. I've had it for years, and I'd bet more than half the people I work with feel it too. They just don't say it. The smarter the team, the less anyone talks about it. It's not hard to see why. Frameworks come and go, languages rise and fall, "best practice" gets rewritten every couple of years, and keeping up basically means always being a little behind. That alone is enough to make most of us feel underqualified for jobs we already have.

Then AI came along and changed the whole flavor of it.

The old version was "everyone here knows more than me." The new version is "the AI knows more than me, so what am I even getting paid for?" Weirdly, I see it more in senior people than juniors. The people using these tools the most are often the ones asking the toughest version of that question. When things come too easy, when the model drafts the insight, suggests the angle, writes the function, you start feeling like the credit isn't really yours. You're not scared of being exposed as bad at your job. You just can't tell where your part ends and the tool's part starts.

That's what hit me. The old impostor syndrome was about confidence. The new one is about identity.

The cognitive debt that makes it worse

This part's harder to talk about, because it goes both ways.

For people like me, AI slowly eats away at the skill. The more I trust it, the less engaged I am. I get the output now, the skill behind it fades in the background, and I pay for it later, when the tool spits out something subtly wrong and I've lost the instinct to catch it. That's the "cognitive debt" people keep talking about, and it's real. I've felt it.

But there's a second effect I care about more, and I don't hear many people say it out loud. AI doesn't just wear down skill in people who had it. It also makes a whole other group of people crazy confident. Dangerously confident, in a way that wasn't really possible five years ago.

Take freelancing. Before AI, if someone posted a job that needed real engineering, the market kind of sorted itself out. Most people would read the spec, realize it was over their head, and move on. The ones who bid were usually decent. If you were serious and going after the hard jobs, you didn't have much competition. Hard jobs went to people who could actually do them. It made sense.

Now look at any freelance site. Someone who's never written a line of code is bidding on that same job, ChatGPT open in another tab. They fully believe AI will carry them. And the worst part? A lot of the time it does. They ship something. It compiles. It looks like what the client asked for. The client accepts it, pays, and moves on, with no idea the person they hired couldn't explain a single line of it. The freelancer takes the money and bids on the next one.

The cognitive debt story misses this. It assumes you had the skill to begin with. A lot of these people never did, and now they don't need it to compete. They're not in debt. They're running on borrowed skill they never earned, and the market can't tell fast enough to punish them.

Once that's normal, the ethics slide right along with it. Nobody's asking if the code is any good. Is it secure? Will it hold up under load? Are the edge cases handled? Can the next engineer even read it? Nope. It's all speed and volume. How fast did you ship, how many features, how many clients this month. Quality became someone else's problem. Usually the next engineer stuck maintaining it, or the customer who eventually hits the edge case nobody tested.

So my impostor feeling isn't totally irrational, but it's not the full picture either. Some of it is real cognitive debt, my skill doing less of the work. Some of it is noise from a market that's stopped rewarding the thing I'm trying to protect. Both are happening at once. The first one I can fix. The second one I just have to tune out.

Two versions I keep catching in myself

Once I started paying attention, I noticed this AI-era impostor syndrome shows up in two different ways. They feel similar, but they come from different places and need different fixes.

Version one: "I didn't earn this." The work came together faster and cleaner than it should have, AI helped at the key moments, and now you can't shake the feeling you were the delivery guy, not the author. This is mostly what Talkspace and Psychology Today write about. It's a credit problem.

Version two: "AI is just better than me." This one stings more. You watch the model write a function in eight seconds that would've taken you forty minutes. You read its draft and it's cleaner than yours. You start wondering why anyone would pay for the slow version. This is a comparison problem, and it gets worse the more you stare at finished AI output without ever seeing it fail.

I think version one mostly goes away once you look at it differently. Version two can be fixed too, but only by changing what you're comparing yourself to.

The thing almost nobody mentions

There's one bit of context that gets left out of most of these conversations, and it matters a lot.

The AI output you see is the polished version. You didn't see the seven prompts that got thrown out. You didn't see the three times the model made up a function that doesn't exist. You didn't see the moment someone realized it had invented a regulation, went along with it, and almost shipped it (CybersecFill on AI hallucinations). You also didn't see the Duke University finding that people who use AI get judged as less competent by coworkers, which is why so many people use it in secret (Axios on secret AI use).

When you compare yourself to "what AI can do," you're comparing yourself to a highlight reel picked by a human who knew which output to keep. That human is still doing the judgment part. The judgment is the actual product. The model's just the typewriter.

That helps me whenever I start spiraling. The model's fast at coming up with options. I'm the one deciding which one's right. Both are real work, just different work.

And once that clicks, most of the guilt about "using AI for everything" goes away. Let it draft. Let it refactor. Let it research. Let it write the first pass of your unit tests. The guilt says the more you hand off, the less of the work is yours. I think it's closer to the opposite. The more easy stuff you hand off, the more time you get for the judgment stuff only you can do. Which is what you were getting paid for in the first place.

The flip side: confident strangers selling courses

One more thing, because this is what finally made me chill out about my own impostor feelings.

(Image was generated by Gemini)

While I'm sitting here wondering if my work is really mine, the internet has grown a whole crowd of people doing the exact opposite. They used AI to ship something that looks like it works, usually through "vibe coding," a term Andrej Karpathy popularized in early 2025 for building software with an AI assistant without necessarily understanding the code it writes (Harvard Gazette). The product runs. The screenshot looks great. And pretty much overnight, the person who shipped it is acting like a senior engineer, a startup founder, and a course instructor all at once.

You can see the loop on any feed. Ship a demo. Post a thread. Launch a course. Sell it to the next person, who does the same thing and sells their course to the person after them. Woke Waves has a writeup on this, and one line nails it: "this person is selling the absence of friction as proof" (Woke Waves). If someone's whole pitch is that there are no hard choices left, no real testing needed, and human skill doesn't matter anymore, that's your red flag.

There's a less polite name for this: the Dunning-Kruger effect of vibe coding. Christian Heilmann's "AI is Dunning-Kruger as a Service" is worth reading in full, but the short version is that AI makes it really easy to overestimate how well you can judge its output, because the output sounds confident even when it's wrong (Heilmann, Genad on Medium). The people who should be the least sure are now the most sure.

I find that funny, and also kind of useful. Funny, because a lot of the loudest people in my feed right now wouldn't pass a basic code review. Useful, because it shows me where my impostor feeling actually comes from. Not from people who know more than me. From comparing myself to a marketing feed made by people who often know less.

And yeah, AI really is that good. It can build almost anything you can describe, as long as you've got the idea. That doesn't make you worthless, even if it feels that way sometimes. The real trap is that it makes the gap between "I have an idea" and "I have something worth charging for" look like it's gone. It's not gone. It just moved. It's sitting in the boring middle now, the parts vibe coders skip. Testing. Edge cases. Security. Customer support. Knowing why the code works so you can fix it when it breaks. The grift only works until someone asks for that part. And eventually, someone does.

So when I start feeling like a fraud, I think about those people. If they can post with that much confidence on that little foundation, I can probably post with a bit more confidence on actual ground.

What I'm doing about it

This is the part I changed my mind on the most while writing this. The answer isn't "stop using AI." It's "use it everywhere, let it do everything it's good at, and use the time you get back to actually get better." I want to be clear about that, because the rest of this post might sound cautious. It's not. Use the tool. Boilerplate, docs, research, first drafts, refactors, that regex you can't be bothered to write. The point isn't to do less with AI. When AI handles the easy 80 percent, you put the 20 percent you saved into the stuff that actually compounds.

The cognitive debt the Microsoft researchers wrote about doesn't come from using the tool. It comes from using it a certain way, the frictionless way, where you accept the output before you understand it. So I split it into scope and autonomy. Scope: go wide, let AI touch everything. Autonomy: keep it tight, stay in the loop on anything going out with my name on it. Here are a few habits I've built around that.

One. I turned off auto-approve. I know, I know. Everyone else is busy building multi-agent swarms, planner agents calling executor agents calling critic agents, all chatting in a Slack channel I'm not even in. And I'm over here doing it caveman style. One agent. One diff at a time. Reading every line before it goes in.

It's not that the fancy setups don't work. For the right problem, they're great. My reason is selfish: every layer of automation between me and the actual change is one more place I stop learning. If one agent writes the code, another reviews it, and a third merges it, I'm not really part of it anymore. I'm a spectator with a job title. The friction isn't a bug. It's where I notice what the model got wrong, where my taste disagrees with it, and where my understanding of the system is weaker than I thought. If the agent had just merged it, I'd have learned nothing and shipped something I only half get. Caveman speed, better output. I'll take that trade.

Two. I use AI to study faster, not to skip studying. In my free time I'm working on certs. Right now it's Microsoft AI-103 and AI-301, plus the new Claude Certified Architect track. AI is the best study buddy I've ever had. It explains something, re-explains it the way I asked, quizzes me, and catches the gaps in my answers. The goal isn't to get through the material faster. It's to understand it better than I would by just reading.

Three. I'm learning AI properly, not just vibing. I don't want to be one of those people from the last section who can ship a demo but can't explain it. I'm digging into how it actually works. How agents are wired up. Where things break. How retrieval, tools, evals, guardrails, and prompts all fit together. Again, selfish reason: if I understand the system, I get to steer it. If I only skim the surface, I'm one model update away from being replaceable.

Four. I keep a private log of decisions, not outputs. Not "what I shipped," but "what I decided and why." The output was often a joint effort with the AI. The decisions were mine. When the impostor feeling kicks in, that log is the best proof I have that the AI didn't do the work.

Five. I watch it fail. I make a point of looking at where AI gets things wrong. Made-up APIs. Confidently wrong math. Code that compiles but doesn't do what you asked. This is the fix for version two from earlier, the "AI is just better than me" one. Stop comparing yourself to the polished output. Compare yourself to everything it produces, broken stuff included. The model isn't better than me. It's faster at confidently giving answers, some of them wrong, and the gap between those two things is exactly where my job is.

Get better every day, with the AI, not in spite of it. Vibe coders are optimizing for the next demo. I'm optimizing for the next five years.

A quick word for people not in the room yet

There's one group I want to call out, because the research says they've got it worst.

Stanford employment data shows software developer employment for 22 to 25 year olds dropped almost 20 percent between 2022 and 2025, while AI coding tools took off (MIT Technology Review). Juniors aren't just dealing with impostor syndrome. They're watching the bottom rung of the ladder get thinner, so every project they land is high stakes, and comparing themselves to senior output (often AI-assisted) feels even more unfair.

If you're early in your career and reading this, that impostor feeling isn't a personal failure. It's just what this moment is like. The way out is the same as it's always been. Build judgment, build context, learn to tell good from bad. Turn off auto-approve. Read the diff. Earn the cert. The model can't do those parts for you, and they're the parts that'll still matter in five years.

Closing thought

(Image was generated by Gemini)

The old impostor syndrome whispered, "you're not as good as people think." The new one whispers, "your good work belongs to the machine."

I don't think either one's right, but the new one's harder to argue with, because the credit really is split now. The question isn't whether AI helped. It's whether the human did the part that mattered. Most days, if I'm honest with myself, the answer is yes. The taste was mine. The decisions were mine. Knowing when to stop and ship was mine.

That's the part the model doesn't do, and it's what I'm getting paid for. The sooner I accept that, and the more I keep sharpening the stuff only I can do, the quieter that voice gets.

Use the tool. Use it for everything you can. Just don't let it use you. Five years from now, I'll be made of the things I stayed engaged with, not the things I let slide. That's the whole post in one line.
