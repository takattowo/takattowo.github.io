Impostor syndrome in the AI age

2026-05-14 9 revisions

The thought that started this post

The other day I shipped a piece of work I was genuinely proud of. It solved a real problem, the code was clean, the writeup was tight. About an hour later, I caught myself thinking, "yeah but how much of that was me". Not as a humble brag. As a quiet, slightly cold question.

I had used AI tools at three or four points in the process. Nothing dramatic. A draft of a function here, a rephrased paragraph there, a sanity check on an edge case. The work was still mine. The decisions were still mine. But the question stuck. If I needed the assistant to get there, do I get to call the result mine.

This is a new flavor of impostor syndrome, and I do not think the old advice quite covers it. So I wanted to write about what it actually is, what the research says about it, and what I have started doing to keep my head straight.

Let me be transparent: I did use AI for this post, mainly for rephrasing and grammar corrections. The ideas, research, and perspective are entirely my own and handcrafted from my point of view.

The classical version, and what AI changed

Impostor syndrome in tech was already bad before AI showed up. I have felt it for years, and from what I see around me, more than half of the people I work with quietly feel the same way. The smarter the team, the louder the silence about it. The reason is not mysterious. Frameworks change, languages rise and fall, "best practice" gets rewritten every couple of years, and the only way to keep up is to stay slightly behind. That alone is enough to make most of us feel underqualified for the job we already hold.

Then AI arrived and changed the flavor of it entirely.

The old version was, "everyone else here knows more than I do". The new version is, "the assistant knows more than I do, so what exactly am I being paid for". I notice it most in the senior people, not the juniors. The ones using these tools the most are the ones quietly asking the hardest version of the question. When success comes too easily, when the model drafts the insight, suggests the angle, writes the function, the human in the loop starts to feel that the credit does not really belong to them. The discomfort is not about being exposed as incompetent. It is about not being able to tell where my contribution ends and the tool's contribution begins.

That is the part that landed for me. The old impostor syndrome was a confidence problem. The new one is an identity problem.

The cognitive debt that quietly makes it worse

Here is the part that is harder to talk about, because it cuts both ways.

For people like me, AI quietly eats at the skill. The more I trust the assistant, the less I stay engaged with the work. I get the output now, the skill that produced it erodes in the background, and the bill comes due later, when the tool produces something subtly wrong and I no longer have the calibration to catch it. That is the cognitive debt people are talking about, and it is real. I have felt it.

But the second-order effect is what I actually want to point at, because nobody seems to be saying it out loud. AI does not just erode skill quietly in the people who had it. It also makes a whole separate population of people insanely, dangerously confident. Confident in a way that did not exist five years ago.

Take freelancing. Before AI, if somebody posted a job that needed real engineering, the market filtered itself. Most people would read the spec, recognize it was over their head, and skip it. The ones who bid were usually decent. If you were the serious person aiming for the hard job, the field cleared for you. The hard job got matched to somebody who could actually do it. The whole thing made sense.

Now go look at any freelance platform. The person who has never written a line of code is bidding on the same job. They have ChatGPT open in another tab. They believe, completely, that the AI will carry them through it. And the worst part is, they are often right. They will ship something. It will compile. It will look like the thing the client asked for. The client will accept it, pay, and move on, never knowing the person they hired could not explain a single line of what they delivered. The freelancer pockets the money and goes to bid on the next one.

This is the part the cognitive-debt story does not capture. The atrophy version assumes you had the skill in the first place. A whole population of people never had it, and now they do not need it to compete. They are not in debt. They are operating on borrowed competence they never had to earn, and the market cannot tell the difference fast enough to punish them.

And once that becomes normal, the ethics quietly slide with it. Nobody is asking whether the code is good. Nobody is asking whether it is secure, whether it will hold up under load, whether the edge cases are handled, whether the next engineer can read it. The conversation is about quantity and speed. How fast did you ship. How many features. How many clients this month. Quality became somebody else's problem, usually the next engineer who has to maintain the thing, or the customer who eventually hits the edge case the vibe coder never tested for.

So the impostor feeling I get is not entirely irrational, but it is also not the whole picture. Part of it is real cognitive debt, the skill doing less of the lifting. Part of it is the noise from a market that has stopped rewarding the thing I am trying to protect. Both are happening at once. The first one I can fix. The second one I just have to learn to ignore.

Two flavors I keep noticing in myself

When I started watching for this, I realised AI age impostor syndrome shows up in two distinct ways. They feel similar from the inside, but they have different causes and different fixes.

The first flavor is, "I did not earn this output". The work landed faster and cleaner than it should have, the AI helped at key moments, and now you cannot quite shake the feeling that you were the courier rather than the author. This is the version Talkspace and Psychology Today are mostly describing. It is an attribution problem.

The second flavor is, "the AI is just better than me". This one is sharper and more painful. You watch the model write a function in eight seconds that you would have spent forty minutes on. You read its draft and find it cleaner than yours. You start asking yourself why anyone would pay for the slower version. This is a comparison problem, and it gets worse the more time you spend looking at finished AI output without watching the failures.

I think the first flavor is mostly fixable through reframing. The second flavor is fixable too, but only by changing what you are comparing.

The piece almost nobody tells you

There is a piece of context that I think gets left out of most of these conversations, and it matters a lot.

The output you see from an AI tool is the polished version. You did not see the seven prompts the user threw away. You did not see the three places where the model hallucinated a function that does not exist. You did not see the moment the user noticed it had quietly made up a regulation, accepted it, and almost shipped it (CybersecFill on AI hallucinations). You did not see the Duke University finding that people who use AI face negative judgments about their competence from coworkers, which is why so many people are using these tools secretly in the first place (Axios on secret AI use).

When you compare yourself to "what AI can do", you are comparing yourself to a highlight reel produced by a human who knew which output to keep. The human in the loop is still doing the judgment work. That judgment is the actual product. The model is the typewriter.

I find this reframe steadies me when I start the comparison spiral. The model is fast at producing options. I am the one deciding which option is correct. Both pieces of work are real. They are different work.

And once that lands, the guilt about "using AI for everything" mostly evaporates. Let it draft. Let it refactor. Let it research. Let it write the first pass of the unit tests. The shame story says the more you offload, the less of the work is yours. The honest story is closer to the opposite. The more of the easy work you offload, the more time you have left to do the judgment work that only you can do, which is the work you were actually being paid for in the first place.

The flip side: the confident strangers selling courses

There is one observation I want to add here, because it is the part that finally made me less anxious about my own impostor feelings.

(Image was generated by Gemini)

While I am sitting around quietly wondering whether my work is really mine, the internet has produced a parallel population of people doing the exact opposite. They have used AI to ship a working-looking product, often through what is now called "vibe coding", a term Andrej Karpathy popularized in early 2025 to describe building software with an AI assistant without necessarily understanding the code that comes out (Harvard Gazette). The product runs. The screenshot is clean. And almost overnight, the person who shipped it starts behaving like a senior engineer, a startup founder, and a course instructor at the same time.

You can spot the loop on any feed now. Ship a demo. Post a thread. Open a course. Sell the course to the next person, who is about to do the same thing, who will then sell their course to the person after them. The Woke Waves writeup on this calls the new scam signal "this person is selling the absence of friction as proof", which I think is exactly right (Woke Waves). If somebody's whole pitch is that no hard choices remain, no real testing is necessary, and no human skill matters anymore, that is the tell.

Researchers have a less polite name for what is going on. They call it the Dunning-Kruger effect of vibe coding. The Christian Heilmann piece "AI is Dunning-Kruger as a Service" is worth reading in full, but the short version is that AI makes it very easy to overestimate your own ability to evaluate the output, because the output looks confident even when it is wrong (Heilmann, Genad on Medium). The people who should be the most uncertain are now the most certain.

I find this funny, and I find it useful. Funny, because the loudest voices in my feed right now are often the ones who would not pass a basic code review. Useful, because it tells me where the impostor feeling actually comes from. It is not coming from people who know more than me. It is coming from a comparison with a marketing surface produced by people who often know less.

And yes, AI really is that good. It can build almost anything you can describe, as long as you have the idea. That is not a threat to your worth, even if it feels like one in the moment. The real trap is that the same property makes the gap between "I have an idea" and "I have a product worth charging for" feel like it has vanished, when in reality the gap has just moved. It is now sitting inside the boring middle, in the parts vibe coders skip. Testing. Edge cases. Security. Customer support. Knowing why the code works, so you can fix it when it breaks. The grift loop only survives as long as nobody asks for that part. Eventually somebody does.

So when I catch myself feeling like a fraud, I try to remember the inverse population. If they can post with that much confidence on that little foundation, I can probably post with a little more confidence on actual ground.

What I am doing about it

This is the part of the post I changed my mind on the most while writing it. The honest answer is not "stop using AI". The honest answer is "use it everywhere, let it do everything it is good at, and use the time it gives you back to actually get better". I want to say that clearly, because the rest of this post might read as cautious. It is not. Use the tool. Use it on the boilerplate, the docs, the research, the first drafts, the refactors, the regex you do not feel like writing. The point is not to do less with AI. The point is that when the assistant does the easy 80 percent for you, you spend the 20 percent you saved on the part that actually compounds.

The cognitive debt the Microsoft researchers described is not caused by using the tool. It is caused by using the tool in a particular way, the frictionless way, where you accept the output before you have understood it. So the distinction I draw is between scope and autonomy. Scope, go wide. Let AI touch everything. Autonomy, stay tight. Stay in the loop on anything you are about to ship under your name. A few habits I have actually built around that split.

One. I turned off auto-approve. I know, I know. While the rest of the internet is busy wiring up multi-agent swarms, planner agents calling executor agents calling critic agents, all of them chatting away in a Slack channel I am not invited to, I am over here doing it the cave-man way. One agent. One diff at a time. Me reading every line before it lands.

The reason is not that the fancy setups do not work. For the right problem, they are great. The reason is selfish: every layer of automation I stack between me and the actual change is one more place I stop learning. If an agent writes the code, another agent reviews it, and a third one merges it, I am no longer a participant. I am a spectator with a job title. The friction is not a bug. The friction is where I notice what the model got wrong, where my taste disagrees with it, and where my mental model of the system is weaker than I thought. If the agent had just merged the change, I would have learned nothing and shipped something I half-understood. Cave-man pace, sharper output. That is the trade I want.

Two. I use AI to study faster, not to skip studying. In my free time I am pushing on certifications. The two on my current list are the Microsoft AI-103 and AI-301, and the new Claude Certified Architect track. AI is the best study partner I have ever had. It explains a concept, then re-explains it the way I asked, then quizzes me, then catches the gap in my answer. The point is not to read the material faster. The point is to land the concept deeper than I would by reading alone.

Three. I am learning AI to the fullest, not just vibing. I do not want to be the person in the previous section who can ship a demo and not explain how it works. I am pushing into the actual mechanics. How agents are wired. Where the failure modes live. How retrieval, tools, evals, guardrails, and prompts fit together. The reason is selfish. If I understand the system, I get to direct it. If I only ride the surface, I am one model update away from being replaceable.

Four. I keep a private log of decisions, not outputs. Not "what I shipped" but "what I decided, and why". The output was often co-produced. The decisions were mine. When the impostor feeling shows up, the log is the cleanest evidence I have that the work was not done by the assistant.

Five. I watch the failures. I deliberately spend time looking at where AI is wrong. Hallucinated APIs. Confidently incorrect math. Code that compiles but does not do what was asked. This is the fix for the second flavor of impostor syndrome I named earlier, the "AI is just better than me" version. The fix is to change what you compare against. Stop comparing yourself to the polished output. Start comparing yourself to the full distribution of outputs, including the broken ones. The model is not better than me. The model is faster at confidently producing answers, some of which are wrong, and the gap between those two things is exactly where my job lives.

Improve every day, with the assistant, not despite it. The vibe coder is optimizing for the next demo. I am optimizing for the next five years.

A short word on the people not yet in the room

There is one group I want to flag, because the research suggests they are the most exposed.

The Stanford employment data shows that software developer employment in the 22 to 25 age bracket fell nearly 20 percent between 2022 and 2025, while AI coding tools rose (MIT Technology Review). The juniors are not just feeling impostor syndrome. They are also seeing the first rung of the ladder thin out beneath them, which means every project they do get is high stakes, and every comparison they make with senior output, which is often AI-assisted, feels even more lopsided.

If you are early in your career and reading this, the impostor feeling you have right now is not a personal failure. It is a structural feature of the moment. The thing that will get you out of it is the same thing that has always worked. Build judgment, build context, build the ability to tell good from bad. Turn off the auto-approve. Read the diff. Earn the cert. The model cannot do those parts for you, and those are the parts that will still be valuable in five years.

Closing thought

(Image was generated by Gemini)

The old impostor syndrome whispered, "you are not as good as the others think you are". The new one whispers, "your good work belongs to the machine".

I do not think either whisper is correct, but the new one is harder to argue with, because the credit really is split now. The question is not whether the assistant helped. The question is whether the human in the loop did the part that mattered. Most days, if I look at the work honestly, the answer is yes. The taste was mine. The decisions were mine. The judgment of when to stop and ship was mine.

That is the part the model does not do, and it is the part I am being paid for. The faster I can sit with that, and the more I keep sharpening the parts only I can sharpen, the quieter the impostor voice gets.

Use the tool. Use it for everything you can. Just do not let it use you. The version of me five years from now is going to be made of the things I chose to stay engaged with, not the things I let slide past. That is the whole post in one line.
